# Source quality

The long form of the ladder in the main body. Read this when a claim is contested or the stakes are high.

## Tier 1 — primary

Official documents, filings, court records, standards, datasets, the vendor's own specification, the contract itself, the page you are looking at.

Checks: is this the current version; is there a date; is it the signed or the draft copy; does the page claim a jurisdiction.

## Tier 2 — first-hand statements

The party's own announcement, transcript, or verified account. Useful for what a party says, not for what is true.

Checks: is the account verified; is the statement on the party's own channel; has it been retracted.

## Tier 3 — expert secondary

Peer-reviewed work, established trade press with named authors, specialist analysts.

Checks: does it cite its own sources; is it reporting or opinion; does the author have a stake; when was it published relative to the facts.

## Tier 4 — aggregators

Encyclopedias, listicles, SEO explainers, comparison sites.

Checks: does it cite a tier-1 source; is the "updated" date real; is the content syndicated from somewhere older.

## Tier 5 — unattributed

Forums, social posts, screenshots, anonymous reviews.

Use as a lead only. A tier-5 item becomes usable when it points to a tier-1 or tier-2 source you have read yourself.

## Common traps

- **Circular sourcing.** Several tier-4 pages repeating one tier-5 original. Count the original, not the copies.
- **Stale facts presented as current.** Prices, availability, rules, and org charts rot. Check the date on the fact, not the page.
- **The confidently wrong summary.** A summary page that drops a qualifier the primary source had. Go back to the primary for any number you will state.
- **Translation drift.** A claim that exists only in translation. Find the original language source before repeating it.
- **Dates without timezones.** "Announced Friday" is ambiguous across timezones; resolve it before you build a timeline on it.
